Funding at the Pace of Adoption
Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS February 2026

1. Introduction & Purpose
The i4i Product Development Award (PDA) is designed to accelerate the translation of promising health technologies into validated, deployable
solutions within the UK health and social care system.
The PDA funds multidisciplinary, multistage projects that generate the clinical, regulatory, usability and economic evidence needed for adoption by
the NHS. Typical projects last up to 3 years, with no maximum finding threshold as NIHR are encouraging ambitious projects, with the potential to
make a real impact.

Core Objectives
•

Deliver robust clinical validation and regulatory readiness for an innovative health technology.

•

Demonstrate safety, efficacy, usability, and cost effectiveness of the technology.

•

Strengthen workforce capacity and pathways to adoption of the technology.

•

Foster inclusive, equitable innovation addressing workforce inequality and digital accessibility.

How to Use This Playbook
•

The Playbook is intended as a companion to official PDA guidance and the NIHR Awards Management System portal.

•

Offers actionable checklists, What Good Looks Like (WGLL) boxes, examples, and embedded templates.

•

Designed for SMEs, NHS trusts, and social enterprise applicants collaborating on translational research.

Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

5. Health Economics
Health economics provides a structured approach for evaluating whether an innovation delivers sufficient benefit relative to cost to justify NHS
investment. For i4i PDA applications, this is essential: decisions must reflect clinical impact, affordability, opportunity cost, and system level value.

5.1 Why Health Economics Matters
NHS budgets face scarcity: demand and available interventions increase faster than funding. Economic evaluation helps ensure resources are
allocated fairly and efficiently, guided by concepts such as:
•

Opportunity cost: using resources for one innovation means they cannot be used elsewhere — a core economic principle.

•

Non-competitive healthcare markets: information asymmetry, limited choice, and lack of price signals mean economic evaluation becomes
essential for rational decision-making.

•

Societal health maximisation: aligning innovation investment with the greatest health gain relative to cost.

•

A clear economic case helps NHS decision-makers and NICE assess whether your innovation is a good use of scarce resources.

5.2 Core Principles of Economic Evaluation
Economic evaluation compares alternative courses of action using a systematic framework:
The Identify–Measure–Value Process

Identify:
•

Resources consumed (staff, equipment, consumables, service use)

•

Outcomes generated (clinical and quality of life changes)

Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

Measure:
•

Quantities of resources used

•

Effects using clinical endpoints or HRQoL instruments (e.g. EQ-5D-5L)

Value:
•

Apply costs using unit tariffs or micro-costing

•

Convert outcomes into cross comparable measures (e.g., QALYs, monetary valuations)

This framework underpins all formal approaches to economic evaluation.

5.3 Types of Economic Evaluation
Different evaluation types answer different decision questions:

Cost Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)
•

Measures outcomes in natural units (e.g. events avoided, pain scores).

•

Most useful when the main outcome can be expressed using a single, straightforward measure.

Cost Utility Analysis (CUA)
•

Measures outcomes in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs).

•

Preferred for NICE-aligned decision-making because QALYs facilitates comparisons for decision-makers between different types of interventions

Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA)
•

Values both costs and consequences monetarily (e.g., willingness-to-pay).

•

If benefits are greater than costs, then the technology is considered to be an efficient use of resources

Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

The playbook recommends CEA + CUA for i4i PDA projects unless strong rationale for CBA exists.

5.4 Choosing the Right Perspective
The ‘perspective’ determines which costs/outcomes are included. Perspectives may include:
•

NHS and Personal Social Services — the NICE reference case.

•

Patient perspective — for capturing burden reduction, travel, time costs.

•

Societal perspective — including productivity, carer contributions.

Choose the perspective that reflects your research question and adoption route and justify it clearly.

5.6 Incremental Cost-Effectiveness & Interpretation
If one technology is not dominant (i.e., less costly and more effective) than an ICER is used to summarise value:
ICER = (Cost_new – Cost_standard) / (Effect_new – Effect_standard)
Understanding uncertainty is crucial:
•

Cost-effectiveness plane helps interpret whether an innovation is cheaper/more effective.

•

Sensitivity analyses (deterministic + probabilistic) strengthen confidence in your estimates.

•

Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) illustrate the probability of the technology being cost-effective for different thresholds for
an additional unit of benefit (e.g., QALYs)

NICE typically uses thresholds (e.g., ~£25–£35k per QALY) to judge whether interventions represent good value.

6. Patient and Public Involvement & Engagement (PPIE) – Working with people and communities
Purpose
Public involvement in research, or PPIE, is defined as research being carried out ‘with’ or ‘by’ members of the public rather than ‘to’, ‘about’ or ‘for’
them. It is an active partnership between the public and researchers. ‘The public’ can refer to patients, members of the public, service users, carers
and community organisations.
When people with different types of lived experience shape research, the innovations and the research process itself are more likely to be inclusive,
reflect real priorities and have meaningful impact on people’s lives.
PPIE is central to the NIHR’s i4i PDA programme. It is currently referred to as ‘working with people and communities’ in application documents.

What should you do and what should you include in the application?
You should involve members of the public from the earliest possible stage, when identifying your research questions and designing your research
plan (at pre-application stage). Early involvement means that plans can be shaped together. You should then make a plan for public involvement
throughout the project. Involvement at all stages can take different forms, ranging from consultation to co-production: to help you plan this work,
you can find more information about these approaches here. You can also find out more about involvement at different points in the research cycle
here. You can use the PPIE Planner to help you describe your plans.
For an outline application, applicants must include information on working with people and communities within the research plan (currently, in the
Methodology/Plan section). You should describe how public contributors have been involved so far – who has shaped this proposal so far, why and
how? You should then describe who will be involved throughout the research/award lifecycle, why and how?
For a full application, you need to build on and add more detail to the plans that you outlined, and justify any changes. You should include a detailed
summary of public involvement so far and involvement plans (currently ‘Working with people and communities summary’), and clearly detail public
involvement in your research plan, too. You should name a PPI Lead who will coordinate this work.
For more details on what to include for both an outline and a full application, please see NIHR’s guidance for applications on working with people
and communities.

Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

8. Research Inclusion
Purpose
Research inclusion (RI) is the deliberate practice of ensuring that research is designed, conducted, and disseminated in ways that represent and
benefit a broad and diverse range of people, perspectives, and contexts. It has been a condition for NIHR funding since November 2024. Addressing
research inclusion in your project is important for several reasons:
•
•
•
•
•

Scientific rigour: using a diverse sample increases the accuracy, translatability and reproducibility of research.
Patient safety, care and health outcomes: treatments, services and initiatives may be ineffective or harmful if differences between groups are
not defined.
Economic impact: less research waste and improved practice and health outcomes translate into positive economic impact at individual
and population levels.
Human rights and ethics: there is a moral imperative to ensure that NIHR’s research benefits all people in society and fulfils everyone’s right
to health and social care.
Legal justification: research that is not inclusive can constitute discrimination under the Equality Act 2010

Along with the Research Inclusion policy, the NIHR has the Sex & Gender policy, which has been active since December 2025. The policy states that
researchers must account for sex and gender in their research, in any datasets they use, and in the data they collect from their participants. More
information can be found here: https://www.nihr.ac.uk/about-us/who-we-are/policies-and-guidelines/sex-and-gender-research

Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

What should you do and what should you include in the application?
1. Embed RI throughout the research lifecycle: Integrate RI considerations from the earliest stages of idea development through to
dissemination and impact.
What to include in your application:
•

A clear statement explaining how RI has shaped your research question and design

•

Examples of decisions influenced by RI (e.g. choice of methods, recruitment strategies)

•

Plans for reviewing RI throughout the project lifecycle

2. Involve public contributors from the start: Work with patients, service users, or community members from the outset and throughout
the project.
What to include in your application:
•

Who your public contributors are and how they were identified

•

Their role at each stage (design, delivery, analysis, dissemination)

•

How you will support and remunerate them appropriately

•

Evidence of early involvement (not just future plans)

3. Include underrepresented and underserved groups: Actively design your study to reach populations that are often excluded or
underrepresented in research.
What to include in your application:
•

Identification of relevant underserved groups for your research topic

•

Tailored recruitment strategies (e.g. partnerships with community organisations)

•

Explanation of how inclusion will improve the relevance and impact of your research

Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

4. Ensure accessibility and inclusion in all project elements: Design your study so that participation is accessible and inclusive.
What to include in your application:
•

Details of accessible formats for participant information (e.g. plain English, Easy Read)

•

Options for different modes of participation (online, in-person, telephone)

•

Consideration of barriers such as disability, caring responsibilities, or digital exclusion

•

Accessibility of venues, platforms, and materials

5. Build a diverse research team: Assemble a team with diverse expertise, perspectives, and lived experiences.
What to include in your application:
•

Description of team diversity (disciplinary, professional, experiential)

•

Roles of team members, including public contributors or community partners

•

How inclusive practices will be embedded in team working and decision-making

6. Justify any exclusions: Critically assess whether any groups are being excluded and ensure this is justified.
What to include in your application:
•

Clear rationale for any inclusion/exclusion criteria

•

Explanation of why exclusion is necessary (e.g. safety, scientific validity)

•

Reflection on how exclusions may limit generalisability

7. Account for sex and gender: Incorporate sex and gender considerations where relevant to your research.
Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

What to include in your application:
•

Whether and how sex will be considered in study design and analysis

•

Whether and how gender (including gender identity) is relevant

•

Justification if sex and/or gender are not considered

8. Plan inclusive and accessible outputs: Ensure your findings are shared in ways that reach diverse audiences.
What to include in your application:
•

A dissemination plan with multiple formats (e.g. plain language summaries, visual outputs, translations)

•

Consideration of audiences with different needs (e.g. children, non-native English speakers, people with disabilities)

•

Plans to involve public contributors in dissemination

9. Budget for inclusion: Allocate sufficient resources to support inclusive research practices.
What to include in your application:
•

Costs for public involvement (including payment and expenses)

•

Translation, interpretation, and accessible materials

•

Accessibility needs (e.g. venues, software, assistive technologies)

•

Training for the research team (e.g. cultural competency, EDI)

What good looks like
Strong RI is embedded throughout the proposal rather than treated as an add-on. Good applications clearly identify underserved groups and show
how their inclusion improves relevance and impact. RI is reflected in the study design through inclusive recruitment, accessible participation, and
appropriate consideration of sex and gender, with any exclusions justified. Meaningful public involvement is evident from the outset, and the team
Dawn Musgrave, NIHR RSS

brings diverse expertise and perspectives. Plans are realistic, with barriers anticipated and resources allocated, including appropriate budgeting for
inclusion. Dissemination is accessible to a range of audiences. Overall, strong applications demonstrate specific, well-resourced actions and a clear
link between RI and research quality, feasibility, and impact.
